Scatter plots can be used to examine the correlation coefficients between variables and thereby infer the associations between them. The upper right part of the matrix displays the correlation coefficients between each pair of variables. The correlation coefficient represents the linear association between two variables. This value is always between -1 and 1, with values close to +1 indicating a positive correlation, and those close to -1 indicating a negative correlation. A positive correlation suggests that as one variable increases, the other tends to increase as well, while a negative correlation suggests that as one variable increases, the other tends to decrease. Moreover, as the value approaches the absolute value of 1, it implies a stronger relationship between the variables. A value of 0 indicates no linear relationship. Typically, an absolute value of 0.9 or above is considered a very strong correlation, 0.7 to less than 0.9 a strong correlation, 0.5 to less than 0.7 a moderate correlation, 0.3 to less than 0.5 a weak correlation, and less than 0.3 suggests little to no correlation. The lower left part of the matrix shows scatter plots of two variables with trend lines. The trend lines indicate the linear pattern between the variables, which is related to their correlation.

If the residuals are well distributed at random around the Y=0 line, and there is no pattern in which the residuals systematically increase or decrease as the fit value increases, the linearity assumption is reasonable. In our case, we conclude that the curve holds the linearity assumption because it is well approximated to the horizontal line.

To see if the homoscedasticity assumption is satisfied, we create a scatter plot of squared residuals as a function of predictor. If the equal variance assumption is satisfied, the square residual does not show a fanning out but instead has a constant variance on the straight line.

#homosedasticity
standardisedResiduals = resid(model)/summary(model)$sigma 

#residual^2 scatter plot

lines(lowess(x = fitted(model), y =sqrt(abs(standardisedResiduals))), col = "red")

The variance of observations should be evenly distributed along the red line above. In other words, the sizes of the upper and lower data should be similar relative to the red line. However, in the case of the graph above, there are parts where the sizes of the upper and lower data are not similar. Differences of around 1.4 among the three datasets or 1.8, 2.2, etc., can be observed in certain parts of the graph. Therefore, it can be concluded that the graph above does not satisfy homoscedasticity.
